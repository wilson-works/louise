# Frost dates for raised beds: overview

> An invented example from Louise's demo library. The sources are made up and live on example.org.

## What a last frost date is

A "last frost date" is the average date of the last spring night at or below 0 °C, worked out from decades of
weather records [^1]. It is an average, not a promise: on the published date there is still roughly a one-in-two
chance of a later frost [^1].

Most tables also give a "10 percent" date, about two weeks later, after which a frost is unlikely in nine years out
of ten [^2]. For tender crops that is the safer date to plan around [^2].

## Why raised beds change the timing

Raised beds drain faster and sit above the cold, wet ground, so their soil warms sooner in spring [^3]. In the
example trials, beds 30 to 45 cm deep reached planting temperature 7 to 10 days before open ground nearby [^3].

The same height works against you on a clear, still night: raised soil loses heat from its sides as well as its
top, so a light frost can bite slightly harder in a raised bed [^4].

## A rule of thumb

- Use the 10 percent last frost date for your area as the earliest planting day for tender crops [^2].
- Check the soil, not the calendar: plant when the bed reads the crop's minimum soil temperature at 10 cm, at
  9 a.m., three mornings running [^5].
- Keep a frost cover by the beds for the first two weeks after planting out [^4].

## In short

Raised beds let Alex plant about a week earlier than open ground, but only when the soil thermometer agrees, and
with a cover ready for a cold, clear night [^3] [^4] [^5].
