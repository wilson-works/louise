# Choosing a used e-bike: overview

> An invented example from Louise's demo library. The sources are made up and live on example.org.

## The battery matters most

The battery is usually the most expensive part to replace, often a third or more of the bike's second-hand value
[^1]. Most lithium-ion e-bike batteries keep about 80 percent of their capacity for 500 to 1,000 full charges [^2].

Ask the seller how far the bike goes on a charge today, and compare that with the range when new [^2]. A bike that
now manages less than two thirds of its original range is likely to need a battery soon [^1].

## A ten-minute checklist at the viewing

- Ask for the charger and both keys; a missing battery key can mean a costly lock replacement [^3].
- Look for swelling, cracks or corrosion on the battery case and contacts [^3].
- Ride it up a hill on the highest assist level: the motor should pull smoothly with no clicking or cutting out [^4].
- Check the chain and rear sprockets for wear; e-bikes wear them faster than ordinary bikes [^4].
- Ask for proof of purchase and note the frame number [^5].

## What a fair price looks like

The example price survey found e-bikes lose roughly 20 to 30 percent of their value in the first year and around
10 percent a year after that [^6]. A three-year-old bike in good order typically sold for about half its new price
[^6].

## In short

Judge the battery first, ride it up a hill, check the paperwork, and expect to pay about half the new price for a
three-year-old bike [^1] [^4] [^6].
